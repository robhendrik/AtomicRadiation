Operator Ordering in the Two-Level Atom Model

The operator-ordering issue is subtle because the full atomic and electromagnetic field operators commute, but their individual free-field and source-field contributions do not.

This is precisely the problem addressed by Dalibard, Dupont-Roc and Cohen-Tannoudji (DDC, 1982).

1. Start with our two-level Hamiltonian

We use the electric-dipole interaction, without the rotating-wave approximation:

$$  
H=H_A+H_F+H_I  
$$

with

$$  
H_A=\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}\sigma_z,  
\qquad  
H_I=-d,\sigma_x E.  
$$

For simplicity, we take the transition dipole along one fixed direction, so $E$ is the corresponding component of the electric field and $d$ is real.

The atomic operators satisfy

$$  
[\sigma_x,\sigma_z]=-2i\sigma_y.  
$$

We want to calculate the rate at which the atom loses energy:

$$  
\dot H_A=\frac{i}{\hbar}[H,H_A].  
$$

Substituting the interaction Hamiltonian gives

$$  
\boxed{  
\dot H_A=-d\omega_0\sigma_y E.  
}  
\tag{1}  
$$

This is an exact Heisenberg operator equation within our dipole Hamiltonian.

At equal times, the complete field operator and the atomic operator commute:

$$  
[\sigma_y(t),E(t)]=0.  
$$

Why? At the initial time they act on different Hilbert spaces, so they commute. Their joint unitary evolution preserves that commutator.

Therefore we can equally well write

$$  
\dot H_A=-d\omega_0 E\sigma_y.  
$$

Or, more generally,

$$  
\boxed{  
\dot H_A=  
-d\omega_0  
\left[  
\lambda\sigma_y E+  
(1-\lambda)E\sigma_y  
\right].  
}  
\tag{2}  
$$

Every value of $\lambda$ gives exactly the same operator.

So far, there is no ambiguity.

2. Now split the electromagnetic field

Solving the Heisenberg equations for the field gives

$$  
\boxed{  
E(t)=E_{\mathrm f}(t)+E_{\mathrm s}(t)  
}  
\tag{3}  
$$

where:

- $E_{\mathrm f}$ is the freely evolving electromagnetic field, including its vacuum fluctuations.
- $E_{\mathrm s}$ is the field generated by the atomic dipole, including radiation reaction.

Substituting into equation (2),

$$  
\dot H_A=\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}}  
+\dot H_A^{\mathrm{RR}}  
$$

with

Equation (4) — Vacuum fluctuations

$$

  

\boxed{

  

\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}}

-d\omega_0  
\left[  
\lambda,\sigma_y E_{\mathrm f}  
+  
(1-\lambda),E_{\mathrm f}\sigma_y  
\right]  
}  
\tag{4}  
$$

Equation (5) — Radiation reaction

$$

  

\boxed{

  

\dot H_A^{\mathrm{RR}}

-d\omega_0  
\left[  
\lambda,\sigma_y E_{\mathrm s}  
+  
(1-\lambda),E_{\mathrm s}\sigma_y  
\right]  
}  
\tag{5}  
$$

Now comes the surprise.

Although

$$  
[\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm f}+E_{\mathrm s}]=0,  
$$

we generally have

$$  
[\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm f}]\ne0,  
\qquad  
[\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm s}]\ne0.  
$$

Indeed,

$$  
\boxed{  
[\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm f}]  
=-[\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm s}].  
}  
\tag{6}  
$$

Consequently, the total rate is independent of $\lambda$, but its two contributions are not.

This is the operator-ordering ambiguity.

3. Why symmetric ordering?

DDC argue that if we want to interpret the two contributions as physical rates of change of atomic energy, each should be represented by a Hermitian operator.

Consider equation (4). Its Hermitian conjugate is

$$  
(\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}})^\dagger  
=-d\omega_0  
\left[  
\lambda^E_{\mathrm f}\sigma_y+  
(1-\lambda^)\sigma_yE_{\mathrm f}  
\right].  
$$

For real $\lambda$, this equals the original expression, in general, only when

$$  
\boxed{\lambda=\frac12.}  
$$

The same argument applies to radiation reaction.

We therefore obtain the DDC symmetric ordering:

$$  
\boxed{  
\begin{aligned}  
\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}}  
&=-\frac{d\omega_0}{2}  
{\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm f}},\  
\dot H_A^{\mathrm{RR}}  
&=-\frac{d\omega_0}{2}  
{\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm s}}.  
\end{aligned}  
}  
\tag{7}  
$$

Here ${A,B}=AB+BA$ denotes the anticommutator.

4. What if we choose another ordering?

Consider the two extreme choices.

Atomic operator first: $\lambda=1$

$$  
\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}}  
=-d\omega_0\sigma_yE_{\mathrm f}  
$$

This contribution is generally not Hermitian.

Field operator first: $\lambda=0$

$$  
\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}}  
=-d\omega_0E_{\mathrm f}\sigma_y  
$$

This is the Hermitian conjugate of the first expression, but is not generally equal to it.

Symmetric ordering: $\lambda=1/2$

$$  
\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}}  
=-\frac{d\omega_0}{2}  
{\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm f}}  
$$

This is Hermitian, as is the corresponding radiation-reaction contribution.

The difference between two choices of ordering is proportional to

$$  
\Delta\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}}  
\propto[\sigma_y,E_{\mathrm f}].  
$$

The radiation-reaction term changes by exactly the opposite amount:

$$  
\Delta\dot H_A^{\mathrm{RR}}  
=-\Delta\dot H_A^{\mathrm{VF}}.  
$$

Thus,

$$  
\boxed{  
\Delta\dot H_A^{\mathrm{total}}=0.  
}  
$$

This explains how different calculations can attribute spontaneous emission differently to vacuum fluctuations and radiation reaction, while predicting the same physical decay rate.

There is an additional distinction between this atomic-versus-field ordering and normal ordering of photon creation and annihilation operators. In the latter convention, vacuum expectations of normally ordered free-field products vanish. Milonni and Smith discuss how this can shift the apparent attribution toward radiation reaction.

5. How does this connect to spontaneous emission?

Once we choose symmetric ordering, we can evaluate the expectation values perturbatively.

The vacuum-fluctuation contribution involves the symmetrized field correlation function,

$$  
C_F(t,t’)=  
\frac12\langle0|  
{E_{\mathrm f}(t),E_{\mathrm f}(t’)}  
|0\rangle,  
$$

while radiation reaction involves the field commutator, through the response function,

$$  
\chi_F(t,t’)=  
\frac{i}{\hbar}\theta(t-t’)  
\langle0|  
[E_{\mathrm f}(t),E_{\mathrm f}(t’)]  
|0\rangle.  
$$

These two functions have different physical meanings:

- $C_F$ describes the strength of the vacuum fluctuations.
- $\chi_F$ describes how the electromagnetic field responds to a dipole source.

Combining these with the corresponding atomic response and correlation functions gives, for the inertial two-level atom in free space, the following leading-order energy-change rates:

|              |                            |                            |                          |
| ------------ | -------------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Atomic state | Vacuum fluctuations        | Radiation reaction         | Total                    |
| Excited      | $-\hbar\omega_0\Gamma_0/2$ | $-\hbar\omega_0\Gamma_0/2$ | $-\hbar\omega_0\Gamma_0$ |
| Ground       | $+\hbar\omega_0\Gamma_0/2$ | $-\hbar\omega_0\Gamma_0/2$ | $0$                      |

For the excited state, both contributions reinforce one another. For the ground state, they cancel.

6. The conceptual point

There are really three separate statements:

1. The total energy-change rate is unambiguous.
2. Its decomposition into vacuum fluctuations and radiation reaction depends on operator ordering.
3. Symmetric ordering provides a particularly natural decomposition in which both contributions are Hermitian and admit an interpretation in terms of fluctuations and linear response.

The third statement is DDC’s central contribution. It does not mean that nature separately measures the two contributions.

For our mathematical backbone, the next derivation should establish explicitly how equation (7) produces the correlation and susceptibility functions, and then the four entries in the table.

That is where the operator-ordering discussion becomes more than a formal mathematical issue: it determines how we can consistently tell the physical story of spontaneous emission and the stability of the atomic ground state.
